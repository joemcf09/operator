  OPERATOR - CHANGELOG

  1.9.0
  -----
    Aircraft Marketplace Generation: Improved aircraft availability weighting based on production history, operator prevalence, age, and secondary-market supply. 
    Reduced repetitive listings of identical aircraft types.
    
    Dynamic Aircraft Inventory: Replaced fixed marketplace quantities with randomized inventory targets, averaging approximately 18 aircraft, with a range of 10–32 and 
    natural listing turnover.
    
    Aircraft Age, Hours & Cycles: Improved used-aircraft generation to produce more realistic manufacturing dates, accumulated flight hours, cycles, maintenance 
    histories, and valuations.
    
    Passenger-to-Freighter Conversion Histories: Converted freighters now retain their original passenger-aircraft manufacturing dates and accumulated utilization. 
    Conversion timing and passenger/freighter operating histories influence hours and cycles.
    
    Additional Freighter Variants: Added 757-200SF, 767-200SF/BDSF, 767-300BCF/BDSF, and 747-400BCF aircraft definitions, including cargo capacities, estimated 
    performance, and operating economics.
    
    Aircraft Performance and Payload Data: Expanded data.py and perf.py with additional freighter-specific payload and performance information to support aircraft 
    eligibility, dispatch, and loadsheet calculations.
    
    Aircraft Registration Generation: Corrected ferry and cargo aircraft registration generation to better reflect the geographical origin of the aircraft.
    
    ACMI Capability-Based Tendering: Changed ACMI requirements from exact aircraft variants to operational capabilities, including aircraft class, type-rating family, 
    cargo/passenger configuration, payload, seating, and sector range.
    
    ACMI Aircraft Eligibility: Added backend validation and frontend eligibility checks showing which fleet aircraft qualify for a tender and why other aircraft are 
    unsuitable.
    
    ACMI Contract Aircraft Assignment: Accepted contracts now record the actual aircraft variant supplied rather than retaining the tender's reference type, improving 
    subsequent flight-performance calculations.
    
    ACMI Utilization Profiles: Introduced maintenance, supplemental, dedicated, and peak-demand contract profiles with different monthly guaranteed block-hour ranges 
    based on aircraft category and customer requirements.
    
    ACMI Minimum Guarantee Accounting: Corrected monthly guarantee calculations, including partial-month proration. Customer underutilization no longer automatically 
    penalizes the operator.
    
    ACMI Contract Performance: Refined sector-completion calculations, performance notices, and minimum-guarantee service credits to distinguish missed scheduled 
    flying from insufficient customer demand.
    
    ACMI Customer Diversity: Improved tender generation to distribute opportunities among eligible airlines rather than repeatedly generating contracts from the same   
    customer.
    
    Realistic ACMI Rotations: Replaced mandatory daily flying with profile-based operating schedules, varying weekly flying days and return trips. Rotations account 
    for aircraft performance, route distances, and operating-time limits.
    
    ACMI Dispatch Interface: Updated the tender, contract, and dispatch screens to show operating schedules and handle non-flying days without incorrectly registering 
    missed sectors.
    
    Dynamic Contract Marketplaces: Replaced fixed ferry, cargo, and ACMI board targets with weekly randomized demand: 8–22 ferry opportunities, 5–16 cargo pairings, 
    and 3–12 ACMI tenders.
    
    Marketplace Save Compatibility: Added persistent weekly board targets and backward-compatible handling of new contract fields so existing save games can continue 
    without a reset.
    
    Real-Time UTC Clock: Updated the interface clock to display continuously updating UTC time with seconds when Real-Time mode is enabled, preserving existing 
    behavior in other clock modes.
    
    Additional compatibility work: Prepared aircraft-type substitution mappings for SimBrief to accommodate conversion variants not directly represented by standard 
    SimBrief aircraft identifiers.
    
    Development status: The principal changes have undergone Python/JavaScript syntax checks and targeted functional testing. Full application-wide regression and 
    integration testing remains outstanding. Some reconstructed aircraft performance values are estimates requiring further validation

  1.8.0
  -----
    Added: type ratings on the air operator certificate. A certificate names the
    types you may operate, and when you found a company you can now have yours
    issued for one of them. Ferry, cargo, ACMI and charter work is then only
    offered on that type, nothing else can be released under the certificate, and
    the crew market fills with people rated on it. If you want the game to be
    about one aeroplane, it now is.

    Adding another type is an amendment, exactly as adding an area is: a training
    programme, a maintenance programme, manuals and a proving flight, so the
    authority charges for it and takes its time. About $36,000 and four weeks for
    a turboprop, $98,000 and seven weeks for a narrowbody, $185,000 and ten weeks
    for a widebody, and the larger classes want a reputation behind them first. A
    second type from a manufacturer already on your certificate is roughly a
    quarter cheaper and quicker, because the training organisation, the maintenance
    programme and the inspector are the same people. Applications are listed on the
    Company profile with the date a decision is due.

    You can still buy or lease an aircraft you are not approved for - the market
    marks it - but it stays on the ground until the type is on your certificate.

    An existing company is unaffected: certificates issued before 1.8.0 carry no
    type restriction, and nothing about them changes.

    Added: business-aircraft ferry work. A certificate issued for a Citation or a
    Gulfstream had no ferry board worth the name, because every ferry job was an
    airliner moving between a lessor and an airline. Business aircraft are moved by
    the people who actually move them: management companies and operators -
    NetJets, Flexjet, VistaJet, Jet Aviation, Luxaviation, Clay Lacy, Solairus and
    others - and the brokers who sell them. The heavy work goes to the
    manufacturer's own service centre rather than an airline shop, at the real
    locations: Gulfstream at Savannah, Appleton, Van Nuys, Fort Worth and
    Farnborough; Bombardier at Dallas Love, Opa-locka, Bradley, Tucson, Wichita,
    Biggin Hill, Berlin, Seletar, Tianjin and Essendon; Textron across its
    company-owned network; Dassault at Le Bourget, Merignac, Geneva, Little Rock
    and Reno; Embraer at Sao Jose dos Campos, Melbourne, Fort Lauderdale Executive
    and Le Bourget; Pilatus at Rocky Mountain Metropolitan. The jobs are the real
    ones: positioning for a scheduled inspection, collecting the aeroplane
    afterwards, moving it for a pre-purchase inspection, delivering it after a
    sale, an import on a new registry, and an empty leg because it is needed at the
    other end. These appear for every company, not only type-restricted ones.

    Added: delivery to base. Buying or leasing an aircraft now asks what you want
    done with it. Have it delivered and the seller or lessor puts a contract crew
    on it and positions it to your base for a fee - fuel, fees at every stop, two
    pilots for the days it takes, their way home and a ferry insurance binder, plus
    the arrangement margin - and it is unavailable until it lands, which takes a
    day or two across a country and the better part of a week across an ocean. Or
    collect it yourself, pay nothing, and fly it home in the simulator as a sector
    of your own. A Caravan from London to Cincinnati is nine legs and six days; a
    747 from Hong Kong is three legs and four. The fleet screen shows what is in
    transit and when it arrives.

    Added: crew files can be renamed, so you can put your own name on the roster
    and mark the file as yours. It shows on the roster, the dispatch sheet and your
    PIREPs. Duty limits, currency and payroll work the same either way; this is a
    first step towards the player being a pilot properly, which needs more thought
    than a name field.

    Changed: a sector handed to the company crew now takes the time it should. On
    the game clock nothing is different - the clock is yours to move, so it files
    at once and the clock jumps by the block time. On a real-time or simulator
    clock the aeroplane is genuinely away: it goes off blocks now, arrives at its
    ETA, and the aircraft and both pilots are committed until it lands. Dispatch
    and the schedule board show it running down, and the PIREP files itself on
    arrival. A sector in the air cannot be cancelled, taken over or filed by hand.
    Several can be in the air at once and they file in the order they land.

    Added: --host and --lan, so Operator can live on a machine that is always on -
    a home server, a Proxmox container, a NAS - and be opened from whichever PC you
    are flying on. It prints the address to use, opens no window and no browser on
    a headless box, and --port moves it off 8123. X-Plane telemetry already works
    across a network, so the simulator can be on a third machine. There is no login
    and no password, so keep it to a network you trust.

    Changed: the simulation is faster again. Overflown countries and route
    remoteness are worked out once per city pair instead of on every job composed,
    route feasibility is cached, and picking a maintenance or storage site measures
    each candidate once rather than three times. A short-range type in a large
    region - a Caravan out of Dubai, a Saab out of Sao Paulo - took seven seconds
    to found a company and run a month; it now takes two.

    Fixed: an empty charter board for an operator whose certificate covered only
    business aircraft or only airliners. The board kept asking for the kind of
    enquiry it could not build and gave up before composing the kind it could.

    Fixed: the cargo and ACMI boards now say why they are empty when the types on
    your certificate are the reason, rather than showing nothing at all.


  1.7.0
  -----
    Added: areas of operation, as an air operator certificate actually specifies
    them. A new certificate authorises the region it was issued in - a company at
    East Midlands may operate in Europe, one at Cincinnati in North America - and
    work is only offered inside it. A sector to a field outside it cannot be
    released; the dispatcher says which area is missing.

    Adding an area is an amendment to the operations specifications: an application
    to the authority with a fee and a wait. The region next door is routine, about
    $38,000 and three weeks. The other side of the world costs around $145,000,
    takes six weeks, and the authority will not consider it below 55 reputation -
    they want a record before extending a certificate that far. Applications are
    listed on the Company profile with the date a decision is due.

    Changed: the simulation is several times faster, and far faster outside Europe.
    Finding the nearest airport to a point scanned all five thousand of them on every
    call, which route planning does thousands of times a day; it now uses a spatial
    index and gives the same answer in a fraction of the time. Runway lengths and
    technical-stop candidates are worked out once instead of on every route, and the
    great-circle calculation caches its coordinates. A company outside Europe took
    three quarters of a minute to advance a week; it now takes a fifth of a second.

    Every airport in the database is mapped to one of seven areas - Europe, North
    America, South America, Africa, the Middle East, Asia and Oceania - and an
    existing company keeps every area it has already been flying in, so no save is
    grounded by the change.

    Added: group charter. Most of the world's charter flying is a whole airliner
    hired by one party, and it is now the second half of the charter business. Ten
    clients who need an aeroplane rather than a business jet: tour operators on
    holiday series, cruise lines whose ship will not wait, football clubs with the
    squad and the kit, government delegations with a press corps, troop rotations,
    pilgrimage groups, fly-in fly-out mining shifts, music tours with the
    production, airlines needing disruption cover within a day, and companies
    taking their staff somewhere warm.

    It is priced the way charter really is: by the block hour for the whole
    aircraft, from about $3,100 an hour for a turboprop to $19,500 for a widebody,
    with the positioning allowance built in. The rates land inside the real market
    bands for every category.

    A group charter needs an aircraft that will take the party - seats, hold space
    for their baggage or their kit - and rostered cabin crew, one for every fifty
    passengers, which blocks the bid outright if you have not got them. Your fleet
    is checked against every brief as before, and the quote dialog spells out what
    the client needs.

    Added: ICAO aerodrome reference codes. Every aircraft now carries its code
    letter from its wingspan - C for the 24 to 36 metre band that covers the 737s,
    A320s, E-Jets and turboprops, E for most widebodies - and group enquiries are
    described in those terms, the way brokers and airports describe them.

  1.6.0
  -----

    Changed: crew names are drawn as whole people rather than a first name crossed
    with an unrelated surname. "Chen Martinez" and "Giulia Petrov" were the old
    method showing through. There are now twenty-seven naming traditions - British,
    Irish, German, Dutch, Nordic, Polish, Slavic, Italian, French, Iberian, Latin
    American, Brazilian, North American, Turkish, Arabic, South Asian, Chinese,
    Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Thai, Indonesian, Filipino, West, East and
    Southern African, and Australasian - giving over four thousand combinations
    that hold together.

    A roster is drawn mostly from the country the company is based in, with the
    international mix any real operator has: a British company hires mainly British
    and Irish pilots alongside Poles, South Asians and South Africans, while one in
    Dubai is mostly Gulf and South Asian. Polish and Slavic surnames take their
    feminine form where they should - Magdalena Kowalska, not Kowalski - and no two
    people on a roster share a first name or a surname by accident.
    Added: several flights can be dispatched at once. Until now one flight had to
    be filed before the next could be released, which made no sense for a company
    with three aircraft. Up to twelve sectors can now be in progress together - one
    per aircraft, and no pilot on two of them - and a "Dispatched flights" panel
    lists them all with what each is doing and who is crewing it. One of them is
    the tracked flight, the sector the simulator is following; press Track on any
    other to move the link to it. The rest can be handed to their crews.

    Changed: the Dispatch page is ordered around the flight you are operating. The
    tracked flight and its map come first, then the list of other dispatched
    flights, and the new-flight form folds away behind "Dispatch another flight"
    once something is in progress.

    Changed: the map is drawn as soon as a flight is dispatched, showing the route
    and the airports along it. It used to appear only after tracking started, so a
    planned sector had nothing to look at.

    Added: an ACMI rotation can be given to the company crew. A contract publishes
    a rotation every day whether or not you have the evening free, so a single
    button on the contract flies today's sectors with your own pilots, keeping the
    block-hour guarantee and the completion figures intact. The flying is scored as
    theirs, not yours.

    Added: a contract can be handed back early. Early termination is charged at
    about a third of the remaining guarantee, up to two months of it, costs
    reputation, and the customer remembers when they next tender. The aircraft and
    crew are released immediately.

  1.5.1
  -----
    Fixed: the dispatch form was laid out badly — the pax, cargo, temperature, wind
    and runway-state fields were far wider than they needed to be, and the last of
    them was pushed off the right-hand edge. Fields sharing a line now divide it
    between them and are allowed to shrink. Checked at window widths from 1,100 to
    1,920 pixels, with nothing running off the page on any screen; the aircraft
    market, which is genuinely a wide table, scrolls sideways within its panel
    instead.

    Fixed: buying an aircraft could leave you with nobody able to fly it. Pilot
    ratings were drawn at random across every type in the game, so a Saab 340 or a
    DC-3 owner could find nobody on the market rated on it. The candidate list now
    always holds two captains and two first officers for each type you operate or
    have taken work on, topped up the moment an aircraft joins the fleet. If the
    market is still short, the crew page explains the alternative: a type rating
    course for one of your own pilots.

    Changed: a new company is told where its first job is. Until you accept
    something, the dashboard carries one panel naming the next thing to do, how
    many jobs are waiting on each board, and the whole sequence from hiring a
    dispatcher to filing the PIREP. Work is never generated on request — it is
    already there — and several people have sat waiting for it to arrive.

    Changed: SimBrief and FSUIPC are made plainly optional. Neither is needed to
    fly: the dispatch form fills itself in from the job, and X-Plane needs nothing
    installed at all. The wording on the dispatch page, the simulator setting and
    the getting-started list said otherwise.

    Changed: starting again is easier to find. Settings has a "Start over" panel
    that explains what deleting the company does and returns you to the setup
    screen.

  1.5.0
  -----

    Changed: cargo pairings are priced the way a crewing contract really is, and
    show it. The block-hour and duty-day rates are now a base, with the premiums
    that genuinely attract more money listed separately: night pay for sectors
    reporting between 2200 and 0500, oceanic and remote pay, a long-haul pattern
    allowance, and special load handling. The board shows what the premiums add and
    the detail dialog itemises the lot.

    Changed: the logbook groups a job's sectors together. A four-sector pairing is
    paid on completion, so showing it as three losses and one windfall was
    misleading; it now has a heading with the job's total and its sectors beneath.
    Turn it off with the tick box if you prefer a flat list.

    Fixed before release: three career objectives could never be completed.
    "Deliver a ferry job and get paid", "Complete three ferry jobs" and the cargo
    equivalents were checked against a counter that was never written, and "Build
    $25 million of equity" called a calculation that did not exist. Deliveries and
    completed pairings are now counted from the record itself, so the objective
    ticks whether or not the job is still on the board — including on a save where
    the work was already done.

    Added: your books can be kept in your own currency. Sixteen are available and
    the right one is chosen from where your company is based - a British operator
    starts in pounds, a German one in euros, a Canadian one in Canadian dollars.
    Aircraft values, lease rates and block-hour rates are quoted in US dollars
    across the industry, so that is what Operator holds internally and converts for
    display.

    Added: work near home. The cargo board now always carries three pairings within
    reach of your base, and both the cargo and ferry boards have a filter for work
    within 1,500 nm of it. A company at East Midlands is no longer shown nothing
    but night freight out of Memphis.

    Added: small freighters and the operators who fly them - the Douglas DC-3,
    Cessna 208B Grand Caravan, Twin Otter, Saab 340B and ATR 42-300F, with West
    Atlantic UK, Swiftair, Jota Aviation, Loganair, Bluebird Nordic, FedEx Feeder
    Europe, Empire Airlines, Mountain Air Cargo, Kenn Borek, Buffalo Airways and
    Everts Air Cargo. Feeder and island work, flown from fields a 737 could not use.

    Added: the Hawker 800XP, with its own type rating, performance and weight and
    balance figures — 2,450 nm, eight seats, 447 knots — following FlightFX's
    release for MSFS 2024. It turns up on the market and takes charter work like
    any other midsize jet.

    Added: simulator substitutions. Every aircraft now says what to fly for it: the
    add-on it is modelled on where one exists, and sensible stand-ins where it does
    not, since nobody owns everything. Shown on the market before you buy, on the
    aircraft record, and in the registration dialog, where you can record which
    add-on you actually fly for that tail — it then appears on the active flight.
    Operator records what you fly, not which product you own.

    Fixed: cargo pairings offered aircraft that have no freighter version, and
    carriers that have no freighters. There is no 787 freighter - a 787 carrying
    cargo is a passenger aeroplane with freight in the hold, which is not a crew
    pairing. Freight fleets are now freighters only: the 757-200PF, 737-800BCF and
    ATR 72-600F have been added, and carriers listed with passenger variants are
    corrected to the freighter they actually operate. The forty-odd cargo
    divisions that only carry freight in the holds of passenger aircraft - United
    Cargo, Delta Cargo, SWISS WorldCargo and the rest - no longer offer pairings,
    while the ones that do run their own freighters, such as Alaska Air Cargo,
    WestJet Cargo, Avianca Cargo and JAL Cargo, keep theirs.

    Fixed: with the simulator set explicitly to X-Plane, MSFS or FSUIPC in
    Settings rather than left on auto-detect, the link never registered as
    connected. Data was arriving and being read correctly — the telemetry was
    there all along — but the connection was never claimed, so the status sat on
    "Waiting for X-Plane" and tracking could not be started. Auto-detect was
    unaffected, which is why it went unnoticed.

    Added: if nothing arrives from X-Plane for twenty seconds, the status says so
    and points at the two usual causes, rather than waiting silently.

    Added: career mode, offered when you establish a company. You start with
    $2.4 million, no aircraft and no crew, and work through six chapters of
    objectives: getting started, learning the paperwork, crewing for the freight
    carriers, metal of your own, contracts and clients, and the long haul. Free
    play is still there for anyone who wants everything open from the start.

    Added: a "Next step" panel that names the one thing to do now, says why it
    matters, and takes you to the right screen. It replaces the old getting-started
    checklist in career mode. An Objectives page shows all six chapters.

    Added: while a company is small, the ferry board always carries three
    straightforward jobs - no tech stops, no oceanic sectors, no deferred defects,
    no permits to wait for, and a week to fly them.

    Added: pilot records. Click a name on the roster for sectors, block hours,
    average and best landings, score, currency by type, occurrences, command
    progress and every sector they have flown for you.

    Added: aircraft records. Click a registration for the airframe history:
    hours and cycles, checks due, engines, revenue earned against direct costs and
    what the lease and maintenance have taken, utilisation by month, most-visited
    airports and every sector it has flown.

  1.4.4
  -----
    Added: aircraft can be re-registered. Press "edit" beside a tail on the Fleet
    page to change its registration, give it a name and a fleet number. The prefix
    decides which authority handles it - a G- registration goes on the UK register
    with the CAA, N- with the FAA, 9H- with Transport Malta - and the cost reflects
    the size of the aircraft, half again on a leased one because the lessor's
    consent is needed. The aircraft has to be on the ground and free of work, the
    registration must not already be carried by another aircraft in the world, and
    the change is recorded in the company history with the previous tail.

  1.4.3
  -----
    Fixed: operators were paired with aircraft by type rating rather than by what
    they actually fly, so anything sharing a rating could turn up with the wrong
    airline - a 767 redelivered to Jet2, who have never operated one, because a
    B757/767 rating covers both aircraft. Every operator now has an explicit fleet,
    aircraft by aircraft: Jet2 flies 737-800s and A321neos, Icelandair 757s, 767s
    and MAX 8s, Delta everything except the 777 it retired, Emirates only the A350
    and the 777. Ferry jobs, ACMI tenders and cargo pairings all draw from those
    lists, and the type ratings shown in the game are derived from them rather than
    the other way round.

    Fixed: a group charter could ask for more passengers than the aircraft has
    seats, which stopped the charter board generating.

  1.4.2
  -----
    Added: developer tools, switched on in Settings and off by default. A panel
    for setting reputation and cash, jumping the clock, adding a job of any kind
    to the boards, refreshing the market, hiring a rated crew pair, making every
    pilot current and rested, and repositioning an aircraft to any airport. For
    trying a feature without flying twenty sectors to reach it. A save that has
    used them is marked, and the status bar shows a "dev" badge while they are on.

  1.4.1
  -----
    Fixed: business aircraft did not appear on the market in an existing save. The
    guarantee that brokers always hold four of them only applied when the market
    went through its weekly turnover, so a save carried over from an earlier
    version showed none until a week of game time had passed - and anyone who
    updated and looked straight away saw an empty list. The market is now topped
    up the moment a save is opened, adding what is missing without removing
    anything you were considering, and a notice says so.

    Added: when the business filter finds nothing, the market says why rather than
    showing an empty table.

  1.4.0
  -----
    Added: the schedule board, under Operations. Every commitment across the fleet
    on one timeline - charter trips, ACMI rotations, the flight currently in the
    air, maintenance under way and checks falling due - with each aircraft as a
    row and a red line marking now. Three, seven, fourteen or twenty-eight days.

    Underneath it, what will not fit. An aircraft in the wrong place for a booked
    trip, with the distance, the positioning time and whether there are enough
    hours left before departure. A deadline you cannot make. No rated captain
    available for a tail that has work on it. Two firm commitments overlapping on
    one aircraft. Each clash offers the fix: "Position it" opens dispatch with the
    trip already chosen.

    Also on the board: work you have accepted that is flown on someone else's
    aircraft - ferry jobs and cargo pairings, with their deadlines - commitments
    that fall beyond the window, and which crew are off the line and when they
    come back.

  1.3.1
  -----
    Fixed: a booked charter could strand itself. The empty leg to reach the client
    was worked out when you won the trip and then never changed, so if the
    aircraft moved in the meantime - for a ferry, a cargo pairing, anything - the
    trip insisted on departing from where the aircraft used to be. Positioning is
    now worked out fresh every time from wherever the aircraft actually is, and
    only the client's own sectors are fixed. Fly the aircraft anywhere you like
    between sectors; the trip finds its own way back.

    Positioning yourself now counts. Fly to the client's departure field on a
    normal positioning flight and the trip simply starts with the paid sector.

    The booked trips panel now shows the client's sectors with those flown ticked
    off, the next sector to fly in bold, and a button that takes you to dispatch
    with the trip already selected - labelled "Position" when the next leg is
    empty and "Fly next sector" when it pays.

    Added: a warning before you bid when a trip needs more than one crew's duty
    day - a four-sector roadshow or eleven block hours cannot be flown by one crew
    without a night stop.

    Fixed: clearer errors when a trip is already complete, and when the aircraft is
    somewhere the trip does not expect.

  1.3.0
  -----
    Charter, in depth.

    The aircraft market and the fleet list now show range, cruise speed, the
    runway an aircraft needs, its fuel burn and hourly upkeep, and the age of each
    airframe - the figures you actually compare on, and essential for judging
    which charter trips an aircraft could bid for. Lease rows show the deposit,
    and the ETOPS column reads n/a on a business aircraft rather than a red No.

    Brokers and competing operators now carry their own marks - Air Charter
    Service, Air Partner, Chapman Freeborn, Avinode, Paramount Business Jets, Jet
    Linx, XO and FXAir on the enquiry board, and NetJets, Flexjet, VistaJet,
    Wheels Up, flyExclusive, Solairus, Luxaviation, GlobeAir, Air Hamburg,
    ExecuJet, Clay Lacy, Gama Aviation, PlaneSense and Airshare when one of them
    takes a trip you quoted for. Sources and licensing for each are in Credits.

    Ten kinds of trip, each flying differently: one-way, day return, overnight,
    multi-city roadshow, tour leg, short notice, medical transfer, group charter,
    long range and event peaks around the Monaco Grand Prix, Davos, the Super Bowl
    and others, where rates rise and slots are scarce.

    Ten client types with their own priorities. An investment fund weighs
    punctuality above everything; a private owner notices the landing; a head of
    state expects both and forgives neither. What they care about decides how they
    score the trip afterwards.

    Twelve requirements that are actually checked against your fleet before you
    bid: seats, cabin size, runway length at every field on the itinerary, range
    without a fuel stop, a cabin attendant, connectivity, baggage volume, customs.
    The quote dialog lists every business aircraft you own and says which can fly
    the trip and what the client will hold against each one.

    Trip costs are yours: handling at every stop, catering per passenger, and crew
    hotels and per diem on an overnight. The fee is not the profit.

    Empty legs: when a trip leaves your aircraft away from base, a passenger may
    ask about the leg home at a fraction of the rate.

    Brokers are real companies - Air Charter Service, Air Partner, Chapman
    Freeborn, the Avinode marketplace, Paramount Business Jets, Jet Linx, XO and
    FXAir - and when you lose a trip you are told which operator took it, from
    NetJets and Flexjet to VistaJet, Luxaviation and GlobeAir.

    Fixed: Send to SimBrief failed on a charter sector. The dispatch form showed

  1.2.1

    straight to them.

    Fixed: listings could be older than the aircraft - a 2009 Falcon 8X, a type

    Fixed: business aircraft were listed with airline utilisation. They now show a

    actually fly, and their cabins are described in seats rather than economy

  1.2.0
  -----

  1.0.9
  -----
    Fixed: a landing could be recorded at an absurd rate - a touchdown flown at
    -150 fpm logged as -6,478. Operator works out whether a simulator reports
    vertical speed in feet per minute or feet per second by comparing what it is
    told against the rate of climb derived from the altitude trace, and a couple of
    bad altitude readings were enough to make it choose wrongly and multiply every
    figure by sixty. The test now needs a clear majority of plausible samples
    before it will decide, ignores implausible ones entirely, and defaults to feet
    per minute, which is what both simulators actually use. Every sample is also
    cross-checked against the altitude trace, so a figure that disagrees wildly
    with what the aeroplane is doing is discarded.

  1.0.0
  -----
  First public release.

    Three lines of business: ferry work for lessors, MROs and airlines; cargo
    contract flying for a hundred real freight carriers; and ACMI wet leasing of
    your own aircraft. All three can run at once.

    Live flight tracking for MSFS 2020 and 2024, Prepar3D (SimConnect), X-Plane
    11 and 12 (UDP datarefs) and FSUIPC, with unit detection so vertical speed,
    attitude and heading are read correctly whatever the simulator reports.
    Landing analysis down to touchdown point and centreline offset, and
    stabilised-approach gates at 1,000 and 500 feet.

    Two-way SimBrief integration: send a pre-filled plan in your chosen units and
    the OFP comes back on its own.

    Runway performance and a full loadsheet and trim document from real runway
    data for roughly 5,000 airports and 12,700 runways, with the limiting weight
    and underload shown.

    Permit paperwork with per-state lead times and readable documents; deferred
    defects filtered to what each type actually has, with enforced restrictions
    and a briefing on how to fly each one; automatic technical-stop routing.

    Crew with type ratings, duty limits, rest, currency on type, command
    upgrades and seniority. Every flight needs a commander; two captains may
    crew together.

    Maintenance including engine shop visits and airworthiness directives; a
    safety model with occurrences, incidents and accidents driven by fatigue,
    deferred defects, maintenance status, weather and runway margin, which can be
    switched off.

    Customers pay on terms, so revenue is invoiced and banked later.

    Analytics with month-end snapshots, a company timeline, printable annual
    reports, and a moving map with real coastlines drawn offline.

    Runs as a desktop application with light and dark themes, a detachable
    flight window, save slots, backups, and 228 operator marks.
